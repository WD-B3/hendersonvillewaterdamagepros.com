Check a Hendersonville, TN Home for Water Damage Before Buying

Water damage is one of the costliest things to discover after closing. This checklist helps you find it before.

By the Hendersonville Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 2026

A home that looks fine on a showing day can hide water damage that was painted over or dried badly. Buyers cannot see inside walls, but they can look for patterns, ask direct questions and bring in the right inspector. This guide is a checklist for a walk-through, not a substitute for a licensed home inspection.

Water history matters because it affects repair cost, insurance and future moisture problems. A single old leak that was fixed properly is a small issue. Repeated leaks in the same place, or a source that was never found, is a larger one.

What to look for inside

Walk through with your eyes and nose open. Look at ceilings, wall bases, floors and cabinets, and look for signs that were covered up.

  • Fresh paint on only one ceiling or wall, especially a small patch.
  • Stains with rings that look like rough circles, even if faded.
  • Baseboards that look swollen, replaced or mismatched.
  • Warped, cupped or stained flooring, or a soft spot near a bathroom.
  • A musty smell that is stronger in closets or the basement.
  • Bubbled paint, peeling wallpaper or cracked drywall tape.
  • Corroded plumbing, rust stains under sinks and mismatched fixtures.

What to look for outside and below

Check the roof from the ground for missing shingles and see whether gutters and downspouts are intact. Look at where downspouts discharge. Check the soil slope around the foundation, and look at the basement or crawl space for stains, efflorescence and rusted metal. Pay attention to the water heater and the air handler, since both can leak.

If the house has a basement or crawl space, ask whether it has flooded and how it was handled. If it has a sump pump, test it with the seller present.

Questions to ask the seller

Ask directly. In many cases sellers are required to disclose known defects, and a written answer is better than a verbal one.

Bring in the right people

A licensed home inspector can use a moisture meter and look for patterns. If the inspector finds suspect areas, a moisture mapping visit can show whether materials are still wet. If damage is confirmed, a written scope of mitigation and repair gives you a number to negotiate with. The wall and drywall repair page and the mold remediation page explain what that work involves.

If you take possession and find active water, call the 24/7 line at once, since early mitigation costs far less than late repair.

Reading an inspection report

Inspection reports use careful language. Phrases such as evidence of moisture, staining noted or recommend further evaluation are signals to follow up and not boilerplate to skip. Ask the inspector to point to each flagged area in person and explain what the meter reading was.

If the report recommends a specialist, schedule the visit before the contingency period ends. A written scope gives you a number to use in negotiation.

Bring a moisture meter or ask the inspector to

A simple pin or pinless moisture meter is inexpensive and quick to use. Check baseboards, window sills, the bottom of walls near bathrooms and the floor under sinks. Compare readings to a part of the house that is clearly dry, since the difference matters more than any single number.

  • Readings much higher near plumbing walls than elsewhere.
  • Readings that stay high on a second visit.
  • Soft or crumbly material that crumbles when pressed.
  • Rust or mineral deposits around fixtures.

Before you sign

Review the inspection, the seller disclosure and any repair records. Ask for written answers to your questions about leaks and claims. If anything is unclear, ask for a specialist visit before the contingency window closes, because discovering a problem after closing leaves you with fewer options.

Standards to ask about

If an inspection finds moisture, ask whether any repair was done to a recognized standard. The industry standard for water damage restoration is ANSI/IICRC S500, and a seller or contractor should be able to say whether it was followed and show the drying records.

What to know in Hendersonville

Check the FEMA flood map for the address you are buying and ask your insurer about flood coverage. The Tennessee Department of Commerce and Insurance says most homeowners policies do not cover flood, and national flood policies usually have a 30-day waiting period, which can affect closing timelines.

The city Building and Codes office at City Hall can answer questions about permits for past work. Hendersonville borders Old Hickory Lake, and the service area page lists the nearby communities if you are comparing homes across Sumner County and the Nashville area.

Local questions

Can a home inspector see mold or water damage behind walls?

A moisture meter and thermal camera can show wet areas, but inspectors cannot see inside every cavity. If a concern comes up, ask for a specialist visit.

Should I ask for a price reduction if I find water damage?

That is between you, your agent and the seller. A written scope and photos give you something concrete to negotiate with.

Will insurance cover a house that had water damage before I bought it?

It depends on the insurer and the history. Ask your agent, and be honest about what you know.
